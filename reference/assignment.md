# Assignment

Condensed from Tomer's email, "Imaginable One Page Simple" and the "Imaginable Mission Builder Guide" (October 2026). Read the originals when wording matters.

## The mission

"Can your classmates escape a world you invented?"

One clickable room: find a clue, then use it to open the exit. Include a fair solution, a hint, a response to wrong guesses, an ending and Restart. 60-90 seconds of play. Children choose their world, a clue or rule, and an ending.

- Lovable inside imagi Edu does the building. The facilitator and short materials give the guidance. A custom platform or automated Gini is outside this job.
- Simple built-in art. No databases, external APIs, online multiplayer, personal uploads or live image or video generation.
- Another child plays on the creator's laptop.
- Theme and mechanics are Kai's choice within this scope.

## Working classroom setup (assumption until Liba confirms)

16 Grade 7 learners, eight laptops in pairs, one facilitator, Chrome, good Wi-Fi, a projector. 45 minutes plus a 15-minute extension. Liba confirms the actual grade (6, 7 or 8), reading and language needs, devices and the student access route.

## What good looks like

- Children want to play. Show the fun first. First playable result by minute 10.
- Each child shapes the game. Their choices change how it plays, and both partners get a turn at the keyboard.
- The guidance helps them think: a clear instruction to AI, a real player observed, a change chosen for a reason and tested.
- Another adult can run it: short instructions, a usable backup, and each child leaves with a next idea.

## Child decisions

- One personal idea from each child before AI offers suggestions. A phrase or sketch is enough.
- No long specification form before they see a game.
- Both partners contribute and explain a choice. Switch the keyboard for the revision. A child may dictate or sketch if writing is a barrier.

## Guidance that teaches

| Skill | Child action | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Creative judgment | Compare two clue ideas for the intended player | "What would each choice make easier or harder?" |
| Clear instructions and AI judgment | Say what should change and what must stay | "AI says it is fixed. What happened when you tried it?" |
| Testing and improvement | Watch a new player, choose a change, predict its effect | "What would show us that this helped?" |

Model the method once, then reduce the help. Later changes use the child's own words. Don't invent a bug.

Teaching standard example, Alien Snack Shop: a receipt says Star = 4, Moon = 2, Rocket = 7. The fridge lock shows Rocket, Star, Moon, so the code is 742. A player misses the receipt. Ask: "Should the hint reveal the code or help them find the receipt? What would each choice do to the puzzle?" One possible instruction: "After a wrong guess, highlight the receipt. Keep the code, ending and Restart." Test: a wrong guess highlights the receipt without revealing 742; 742 opens the fridge; Restart clears the highlight; a new player tests whether the clue is easier to find.

## Run sheet (starting point)

| Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0-2 | Show the game; invite children to create a world someone else can escape |
| 2-5 | Each child does the starting check independently |
| 5-10 | Each partner contributes an idea; send the first build instruction; while it loads, predict one thing the game should do |
| 10-17 | Try the first game; personalise a clue, rule or ending in their own words |
| 17-23 | Neighbouring pairs play each other's games on the creator's laptop; creators watch without giving the solution |
| 23-31 | Each child proposes a change; choose one for a reason; switch keyboard; ask AI to make it |
| 31-35 | Test the change and something that should still work; use an untold player when judging clarity |
| 35-38 | Each child does the other independent check |
| 38-45 | Save, maker note, choose a next upgrade; keep time for delays |

- One action at a time on screen, plus a short help card. "Change __. Keep __. Check __." only when needed.
- A tested way back to a working version.
- No game by minute 12: the pair continues on the paper version while the facilitator fixes access.
- The extra 15 minutes are for support or an upgrade, never for finishing the core.

## Sharing

- Invitation: "Can you escape my moon bakery?" After play, the creator explains one choice; ask what the player would make differently. Polite praise is not a signal that they want to create.
- Take-home card: the game, one child-made decision, their next idea.
- A play-only link only if agreed and tested outside the creator account, with an adult managing onward sharing. An unlisted link is not private access. Otherwise a screenshot. Record external play as untested. No sharing infrastructure.

## Learning evidence

Two capabilities: giving AI a clear instruction from an idea, and using a real test to judge and improve the result.

Maker note (learner number; a saved prompt or sketch can stand in for writing):

- My idea was __. We could try __ or __. I chose __ because __.
- I told AI to change __ and keep __.
- The player did __. We changed __. The next test showed __.
- Next I want to add __. My first step would be __.

Two independent checks, same questions, up to three minutes each, no AI or partner help. Odd learner numbers do A then B; even do B then A. Record reading support; keep the first answer before coaching.

- A, Quiz game: a correct answer should add one point, a wrong answer none, Restart sets the score to zero. A test shows a wrong answer adds one point. AI says "Done. The game works."
- B, Treasure game: finding treasure adds one point, an empty chest none, Restart sets zero. A test shows an empty chest adds one point. AI says "Done. The game works."

Questions: 1. What does this test show, and what is still unknown? 2. What would you tell AI to change and keep? 3. What would you try next, and what should happen?

| Check | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reason | No relevant reason | Spots the failure | Uses the result and names an untested rule |
| Instruction | Vague or breaks a rule | Specifies the change | Specifies the change and what to keep |
| Test | Only "test it" | An action and expected result | Checks the fix and a kept rule, with expected results |

Report counts and a few examples. A pair's game is not an individual score. These are classroom checks, not validated measures.

## Final handover (15 paid hours in total)

Suggested split: 3 early build and review; 5 game and lesson improvements; 4 materials and tests; 3 handover, feedback and one revision. Flag overruns early with the smallest useful cut.

One folder with a Start Here index and editable files:

| Part | Contents |
|---|---|
| Student materials | 6-8 short slides or an equally clear guide; an editable first-prompt scaffold with room for child ideas; one help card; both checks; the maker note |
| Facilitator materials | Setup and timed run sheet; what to say; partner turns; help and recovery steps; scoring guide and observation sheet; a printable paper backup; take-home card |
| Working example and evidence | Playable example and project access; key prompts; before and after views; short walkthrough; test results, waits and credits used; reused assets and sources; known blockers |

Checks before handover:

- Two contrasting worlds through the same lesson, with a different clue or rule. Polish one, keep the second rough.
- The student route: build, revise, save, close, reopen, edit; recovery after a failed change. Don't rely on educator access or an untested undo.
- The full player path: clue, wrong or empty guess, hint, correct answer, ending, Restart. Check the real destination of every main button.
- Time the class flow, including AI waits, partner changes and saving. Record credits. Repeat facilitator prep under 20 minutes once access works.
- Paper backup: printable room, clue, lock and answer key. Children still choose, play, change and retest. Record paper use separately.
- Tomer or the facilitator follows setup, runs a build or revision and uses a help prompt without Kai taking over.

## Judging

A game children want to play, choices that stay theirs, guidance that draws out useful thinking, and a kit one adult can run. Testing, tradeoffs and response to feedback matter. Say what failed. At the three-hour review they decide: continue, narrow or stop.
